A whole-course design · ECON 2316 example

A Five-Step AI Lab Sequence

Instead of a little AI in every assignment, one 40-minute lab per unit, each teaching one AI skill on that unit's theory. By the end, students have practised the whole cycle: ask, check, generate, argue, and decide when not to use it.
The pattern (any discipline): pick five skills you want students to leave with, give each one a short lab tied to a unit, and keep three rules in every lab: paper first (the student's own answer is on record before the AI speaks), a time cap, and a no-AI alternative for full marks. Students are graded on what they did with the AI, not on the AI's answer.
1

How to ask

Unit 1 · supply, demand, elasticity

Students write what they think elasticity is, then compare a vague prompt with one that gives context, a task and a format. Last, they make the AI question them before it explains anything.

2

How to check

Unit 2 · consumer choice

Four quick checks a correct answer must pass. The AI plants an error; students find which check caught it, and which one passed anyway. See this lab in detail →

3

How to generate

Unit 3 · costs, right before the exam

Students write a spec for practice problems, attempt two by hand before seeing solutions, then audit all six: is each one solvable, unambiguous, on topic, with sensible numbers? They keep the repaired set for revision.

4

How to argue

Unit 4 · market power and strategy

Students commit to a position on a real merger, then make the AI build the strongest case against them. They answer the hardest argument with course tools (market definition, concentration, entry) and say honestly whether they moved.

5

When not to use it

Unit 5 · risk, information, externalities

Students run four real AI uses from their semester through three questions from the unit: what would a wrong answer cost, could a reader tell, and was it worth the cost to others? They write three specific rules and have the AI try to break them.

AI-THINK

Checking and arguing with AI output, using the course's own theory.

AI-DATA

Seeing where fluent answers fail: planted errors, broken practice problems.

AI-ETHICS

Deciding when AI should stay out, by reasoning, not by rule.

AI-CREATE

Students build things they keep: a prompt habit, a checked problem set, their own rules.

Adapt it: keep the five skills and swap the content. Writing: ask for feedback on a draft, check a summary against the source, generate counter-arguments, argue a thesis, and decide which writing tasks stay yours. History or policy: the same five, with documents and briefs. One skill per unit, paper first, and a no-AI alternative every time.
