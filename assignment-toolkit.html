Assignment Toolkit

Make an assignment AI-resistant while keeping your learning goals. Pick your assignment type — you don't need every fix; one or two meaningfully reduces vulnerability.

Is this assignment AI-vulnerable?

30-second diagnosis
  • Could a generic AI tool answer it well without doing the reading?
  • Could a student answer from general/news knowledge instead of the specific material?
  • Does it only ask “what do you think / what resonates?”

If yes to any, pick your assignment type above for concrete fixes.

Discussion prompts & qualitative work

The Four Levers — from Linda Kowalcky's checklist
1 · Anchor to something AI can't know — a personal/local/specific referent (a real policy, event, dataset).
“Name a real policy in your life; apply the principle to it.”
2 · Trace the “why”, not the “what” — require the mechanism, not a summary.
“Pick one design element; explain why that one had to change.”
3 · Build in peer dependency — the follow-up needs classmates' specific input.
“Compare examples across your group; report the best pair.”
4 · Require a textual reference — a page number / quoted term in the pre-discussion post.

Papers & essays

Built for non-writing courses with no in-class draft time
Anchor to the text + your framing — require named passages (page numbers) and a concept from your own lectures. AI can't know how you framed it.
Add a light process memo (½–1 pg) — thesis, what changed as you read, the turning-point passage. Quick to skim, hard to fake.
Add an oral beat — a 3–5 min “walk me through your argument” (office hours, class slot, or 2-min video).
AI-as-source-to-critique — students run their prompt through AI, then critique what it got wrong about the specific text.

Quantitative / data assignments

Grade thinking, not output
Grade interpretation — AI can produce the regression; require interpreting, critiquing, and justifying specification choices.
Anchor to a chosen dataset/case + a decision log (“why this method, why this variable”).
Argue with the AI — students find the model's errors or bad assumptions.
Verify live — a short in-class replication or explain-your-code moment.

Moves that work on any assignment

Separate practice from assessment — practice low-stakes; put high stakes where you can verify (in-class, oral, proctored).
Grade process + product — drafts, reflections, version history, checkpoints.
Protect the early, generative stage — first independent thinking before AI enters.
Make AI expectations explicit — and explain why — what skill the task builds, why the shortcut bypasses it.

Patterns that work

Field-tested structures you can adapt to any discipline
Manual-first, then AI — students do the core by hand first, and AI unlocks only after, with their use documented: what did you ask, and how did you verify it?
Anchor the grade to something you can see — pair take-home work with a proctored quiz or a short in-class checkpoint.
Authentic project + short oral defense — a real-data or replication project the student must defend live.
The oral / replication interview — a 10–15 min conversation where the student explains choices in real time. Verifies authorship; doubles as interview practice.
Argue with the AI — students critique AI output that's subtly wrong and catch the flaw.
Live in-class activities — polls, simulations, games: content that only exists after class meets can't be AI-faked.
Grounded in Northeastern's AI Readiness Framework and the “Assessment in an AI-Enabled World” faculty guide. Discussion-prompt levers adapted from Linda Kowalcky's checklist.
